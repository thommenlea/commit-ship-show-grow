The model on one page

The model on one page

The brief, as I read it. Gagan, you've talked about having to design your own path in college, and doing it listlessly. That is the design brief. HAA keeps the freedom and removes the listlessness. Fellows choose what they build. The Academy supplies the structure, the peers, and the bar that turn self-direction into growth.

Students own the work.

Every Fellow runs their own Pursuits and sets their own goals. Nobody assigns a project.

The community owns the bar.

Standards come from peers, visible work, exemplars, and candid critique. Staff model the bar. They don't police it.

Reality does the grading.

Users, customers, practitioners, and results judge the work. Growth is assessed from evidence and never graded.

01Backward design

Start from the graduate

I design backward: define what the learner should be able to do, decide what evidence would convince us, then design the experiences. That is Backward Design (Wiggins and McTighe), the method I've taught to more than 400 future educators. For HAA it means turning four abstract traits into behavior we can see.

By August 2028, every Founding Fellow can show:

  • Shipped work that real people used, with numbers attached.
  • 100 or more real asks (cold emails, calls, user interviews, pitches) and what came of them.
  • At least six public presentations of their work, and the feedback that changed it.
  • At least one thing they built that would have needed a team before AI.
  • Three months of real work inside a company, reviewed by their manager.
  • A written account of how they grew, backed by evidence a stranger would find credible.

02Structures for self-direction

The operating system: structures students run

Self-direction needs more structure than lectures do. Research on minimal guidance is clear that freedom without structure produces drift, especially for people new to a field (Kirschner, Sweller and Clark, 2006). What changes at HAA is whom the structure serves. Every structure below helps a Fellow make and keep their own commitments, and Fellows run most of them.

A Show Week sits between cycles: Exhibitions, Growth Reviews, rest, and planning. Rest is part of the design, because ambition burns out when it's run as a sprint.

Co-ops. I come from Switzerland, where about two-thirds of teenagers learn a trade through apprenticeships split between a company and a school. The lesson from that system: learning at work succeeds when the learner, the company, and the school share one framework. Every HAA co-op starts with a learning contract (two growth edges on the rubric, agreed with the manager), keeps a short weekly Ship Log, gets manager check-ins on the same rubric, and ends with a Co-op Debrief in SF.

Breakouts. If a Pursuit takes off with users or funding, the Fellow stays and the rituals adapt to founder mode. HAA challenges dropout culture, and success shouldn't be a reason to leave.

The cycle (six weeks)

  • Week 0, Pursuit Charter. One page, written by the Fellow before any AI help: what will be true in six weeks if this goes brilliantly, the audacious version, one metric, the riskiest assumption, who I need, what I want to learn. Reviewed with the pod and the coach.
  • Weeks 1 to 5: build, inside the weekly rhythm.
  • Week 6, Exhibition and Growth Review (sections 3 and 6).

The 100 Asks. Every Fellow logs their asks in the Ship Log: cold emails, user interviews, pitches, requests for help. The target is 100 over the year. Asking is the one rep that builds agency and people skills at the same time, and most young people have never done it at volume.

Courses stay pure input. I'd keep your principle that Courses feed Pursuits indirectly: no Course assigns deliverables, and Fellows decide what to use. The bridge is one question at Pod Commit: "What from this week's Course are you stealing?"

AI does the tutoring. Humans do the pushing. Erik Torenberg's announcement cites Bloom's finding that one-on-one tutoring beats 98% of a conventional class. AI makes the tutoring half affordable for every Fellow. Each Fellow gets a Pursuit Copilot, an AI assistant grounded in their Charter and Ship Log that teaches what the Pursuit needs next, when it's needed. Coaches and mentors are then free for judgment, motivation, and the bar. I've built this kind of assistant before: it guided more than 100 of my students through course content and their capstone projects. Fellows own their data.

Mentorship touchpoints: the ladder

HAA's network holds more than 200 practitioners, and their time is scarce. Each touchpoint is a defined format, so a small amount of time counts:

  1. Answer an Ask: async, five minutes.
  2. Office hours: 30 minutes, bookable. The Fellow brings one decision.
  3. Teardown: a recorded ten-minute critique of a product, pitch, or paper.
  4. Panelist: 90 minutes at a crit or Exhibition, once per cycle.
  5. Pursuit mentor: three sessions with one Fellow across one cycle.

Fellows pull mentors. Coaches make sure nobody goes a cycle without at least one practitioner touchpoint, because the Fellows who most need a mentor are often the least likely to ask.

Fellows run it

  • Fellows facilitate pods and crits from Cycle 2 on.
  • Fellows write the HAA Standard (section 3).
  • Any Fellow can propose a new ritual. If eight Fellows join, it runs for a cycle and stays only if it earns its time.
  • Every Fellow teaches one session per cycle: a skill, a tool, or a teardown of their own failure. Teaching spreads knowledge through 50 people without lectures.
  • Once the two-year program starts, second-years coach first-year pods and sit on admissions panels.
03Peer norms and osmosis

The bar: set by peers, not enforced

What the bar is. The reaction admissions looks for, applied every week: "You did what?" There is no discount for age. A Fellow's app is compared with apps, and their research with research.

Seven ways the bar spreads without enforcement

  1. 01Select for it. Admissions and the Dean share one trait rubric, so HAA admits people who raise the average.
  2. 02Set it in week one. Teams settle their working patterns early and keep them until something disrupts them (Gersick, 1988). Launch Week ends with every Fellow shipping something real, so the first norm anyone experiences is that everyone ships.
  3. 03Make work visible. Ship Logs, the Friday digest, a wall of live metrics, public Exhibitions. When you can see what 49 peers shipped this week, nobody has to tell you your week was thin.
  4. 04Show exemplars. A library of excellent work: first versions of products that became great, the bodies of work the Courses study, and later the best work of earlier Fellows. People learn what "good" means from examples far better than from rubrics (Ron Berger, An Ethic of Excellence).
  5. 05Critique that cuts. Pixar's Braintrust rule: notes are candid, and the owner decides what to do with them. HAA's crit protocol is Show → Ask → Push → Own. The presenter shows the work and asks for the feedback they need. The panel pushes, always including "What would the 10x version be?" and "How do you know it works?" The presenter then says publicly what they'll do next. Feedback targets the work, never the person. You believe in feedback in public, and this format makes public feedback safe enough to be honest.
  6. 06Fellows write the Standard. In Cycle 1, the Founding Class writes the HAA Standard, one page defining excellent work and how we treat each other, and revises it every cycle. Communities keep the rules they make themselves (Elinor Ostrom).
  7. 07Builders in the room. Builders in Residence, and co-working tenants who trade affordable space for open doors and demo attendance, if Operations builds the co-working floor. Newcomers learn most by watching people a few steps ahead (Lave and Wenger).

What we celebrate. Status goes to what we want more of: shipping, audacious attempts, and helping others ship ("Unblocker of the week" in the digest). Failure gets a debrief: honor the attempt, extract the lesson, publish the post-mortem. That matches your view that failure earns congratulations on the attempt and a real lesson, without being romanticized.

When a Fellow stalls. No punishment. Graduated support, faster than any grading system could react:

  1. The Ship Log makes a stall visible within two weeks.
  2. Pod check-in: peers ask what's going on.
  3. Coach reset: diagnose whether it's scope, skill, motivation, or wellbeing.
  4. A two-week reset sprint with small, sure commitments to rebuild momentum.
  5. If it persists, a candid conversation about fit, held with care.
04Push, then step back

Coaching: push bigger, know when to step back

Erik's announcement promised "dedicated, experienced coaches and mentors who know your work and psychology." My coaching model rests on one finding from student development research: people grow when challenge and support are both high (Nevitt Sanford). The coach reads which state a Fellow is in and makes the matching move.

Two questions do most of the work. "What would the 10x version be?" builds ambition. "What's the smallest version you can ship in seven days?" builds agency. Good coaching alternates between them.

Knowing when to step back. The signals: the Fellow solves their own problems, makes reasoned trade-offs, and asks better questions than the coach. Then the coach fades. Cognitive apprenticeship research names the sequence: modeling, coaching, scaffolding, fading (Collins, Brown and Newman). Each Fellow also sets a coaching contract at the start of the year: how hard they want to be pushed, and how they want to hear it.

How hard feedback lands. One sentence changes how people take criticism: "I'm giving you this feedback because I have very high expectations and I know you can reach them." In a randomized study, feedback framed this way made students much more likely to revise their work (Yeager and colleagues, 2014). Every coach and crit lead uses it. It is how you hold an extremely high standard and care deeply about someone at the same time.

The whole person. Fellows will be 16 to 22, many away from home for the first time, surrounded by intense peers and famous people. Built in from day one: reset weeks, a wellbeing check in every 1:1, a clinical partner with a clear referral path, and safeguarding for Fellows under 18. Coaches are not therapists, and they know exactly when to call one.

Who I hire

  1. Has built something real. Fellows respect people who have shipped.
  2. Coaches instead of fixing. Asks the question that lets the Fellow find the answer.
  3. Is a warm demander: high standards and obvious care at once. The term comes from research on the teachers who got the most out of their students (Kleinfeld, 1975).

How I test for it. Interviews don't show coaching. Every finalist runs a live crit with real young builders, coaches one of them for 20 minutes, and writes a growth narrative from a real Ship Log. I hire for what I see.

How I train them

  • Coach bootcamp (two weeks, August 2027): the model, the rubric, the crit protocol, wise feedback, safeguarding. Practiced with pilot students.
  • Weekly calibration: coaches rate the same Fellow evidence independently, then compare and resolve differences. It keeps judgment-based assessment fair, and it's how I calibrated scoring criteria for teacher certification exams.
  • Observation cycles: coaches record and review their own sessions, with consent, the way I coached more than 20 early-career teachers through video-based feedback.
  • A living playbook, updated every cycle, so the model survives growth and turnover.

Making 200+ busy practitioners useful. Most operators are brilliant and have never designed learning. Course faculty get a one-page design kit and a 45-minute session with me: open with a real problem you faced, study the real artifact, let Fellows interrogate you, and leave them with a question to carry into their own Pursuit. Mentors get the ladder and a two-minute brief on each Fellow before any session. Co-op managers get the rubric and a 20-minute orientation.

06Traits that can't be graded

Assessment: growth on traits that can't be graded

You can't test agency, but you can watch it. The traits are invisible. The behavior they produce isn't. So HAA assesses behavior, from evidence, against each Fellow's own starting point.

Seven rules

  1. 01Evidence, not impressions. Every judgment cites something: a Ship Log entry, an email thread, a user metric, a recorded demo, feedback a peer rated as useful.
  2. 02Triangulate. Four sources for every trait: reality, peers, coaches and mentors, and the Fellow.
  3. 03Growth against your own baseline. Measured in Launch Week, remeasured every cycle.
  4. 04Narratives, not numbers. A written account with evidence links. No grades, scores, or GPA.
  5. 05No ranks, no leaderboards. Comparison happens through visible work, never through an official ladder.
  6. 06Guard against Goodhart's law. When a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring. Indicators inform judgment and never become quotas or rewards.
  7. 07The Fellow writes first. Every review starts with the Fellow's Growth Memo, drafted before any AI help, in line with your first-draft rule.

Feedback on feedback. After every crit, the presenter rates each piece of feedback with one tap: useful or not. Over a year, that builds a real record of who gives feedback people act on, which is the core of people skills.

The rhythm

  • Launch Week baseline: the first build, a structured interview, and a self-assessment on the rubric. Each Fellow picks two growth edges.
  • Every cycle: an Exhibition with a practitioner panel (the demo, then a defense of the choices behind it), followed by a Growth Review: the Fellow's Growth Memo (two pages at most), the coach's narrative, peer and mentor input, a 45-minute conversation, and new growth edges.
  • Co-op: the learning contract, with manager check-ins on the same rubric.
  • August: the Fellow Record.
Growth Review · Cycle 3Invented Fellow, for illustration

Agency: Practicing → Strong. In Cycle 1, Fellow A kept 5 of 9 weekly commitments and sent every blocker to their coach. In Cycle 3, they kept 11 of 12, sent 140 cold emails to clinic managers (38 replies, 6 pilots), and got past a data-access refusal by asking a mentor for an introduction to a different clinic.

Next edge. They still avoid asks where rejection feels personal, like investors and senior engineers. Cycle 4 commitment: ten asks to people they believe are out of reach.

The Fellow Record. HAA promised a portfolio in place of a degree. I'd make it the most credible document a 19-year-old can hold: shipped work with results, a growth narrative on each trait with evidence links, Exhibition recordings, and signed endorsements from practitioners and the co-op manager. It serves three readers. Employers and partners use it to hire. Colleges use it, since HAA believes in college and some Fellows will go. Regulators use it: when HAA seeks approval for the two-year program, this system documents learning outcomes without a single grade. I've translated accreditation requirements into competency roadmaps before, and I'd build the record with that approval in mind from day one.

Is the model working? A short monthly dashboard for you

  • Weekly: share of Fellows who shipped to a real user, commitments kept, asks made, and a two-question pulse: "This week I was stretched" and "This week I had the support I needed."
  • Every cycle: movement on the rubric, panel ratings at Exhibitions, mentor touchpoints per Fellow, stalls caught and recovered.
  • Long term: co-op manager ratings and return offers, then what Fellows build at one, three, and five years.

Every ritual is a hypothesis with an owner and a review date. If it doesn't earn its time, it goes.

08Pre-mortem

What could go wrong

It's June 2028 and the model has failed. The likeliest reasons, and what I'd build against each:

FailureGuardrail
Freedom became driftSix-week cycles, weekly commitments, stalls visible within two weeks
Public feedback became performance or crueltyThe crit protocol, the owner decides, feedback on the work only, feedback trained as a skill
Intensity became burnoutReset weeks, wellbeing checks in every 1:1, a clinical partner, pace norms in the Standard
Everyone built the same AI startupAn explore phase first; research, hardware, nonprofits, and media honored equally
Famous mentors, thin mentorshipDefined formats; depth comes from Resident Coaches and Builders in Residence
Metrics got gamedNo ranks or leaderboards; evidence informs judgment and never becomes a quota
It worked for 50 and couldn't growPeer-run rituals and software from day one; a written playbook; second-years coaching first-years

The last row answers the critics' strongest point, that a model built for 50 handpicked students won't scale. A system run by peers gets stronger as it grows. A system run by staff only gets more expensive.

09Fit

Why me

I'm not a founder, and HAA doesn't need its Dean to be one. Its network is full of founders. It needs someone who knows how people grow, can build the weekly system that makes growth happen, and can prove that it did. That has been my work for nearly a decade.

  • I've assessed complex work with rubrics and defenses instead of answer keys. I designed certification exams for more than 100 teacher candidates: a rubric-scored design submission plus a structured oral defense, with calibrated criteria and pass thresholds. The Exhibition and Growth Review in this sketch are that system, rebuilt for builders.
  • I've taught people to design their own learning. At FHNW I trained more than 400 future educators to design competency-based learning, with a 95% mastery rate and a capstone in which each designed their own learning architecture.
  • I've coached. More than 20 early-career teachers through video-based observation and feedback cycles, and more than 10 new teachers as their mentor.
  • I've trained the people who teach. Professional development workshops for more than 100 practicing teachers on designing high-engagement learning, rated 4.5 out of 5.
  • I've led teams and measured impact. I led curriculum development for a nationally funded Swiss curriculum with a team of 10 designers and educators, and measured a 40% increase in competency acquisition across a 200-learner pilot, which I reported to the national funders.
  • I build with AI. A custom AI assistant for more than 100 students; Claude Code agents that faculty ran on their own, part of a workflow change that cut content production time by 30%; and my own multi-skill agent system with automated quality gates.
  • I know teenagers. Eight years teaching more than 1,000 secondary school students.

I live in San Francisco, and I'm ready to be all in. I'd love to build this with you.
